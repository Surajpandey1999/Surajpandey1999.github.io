Reservation in India: Justice, Representation or Political Strategy?

From the constitutional promise of social justice to a contested arena of electoral politics — what the Constitution, the courts and the data actually say, without picking a side for you.

FILED BY SURAJ PANDEY · POLITICAL SCIENCE & PUBLIC ADMINISTRATION

Justice Representation Politics
11 min read • Updated 25 Aug 2026 • 8 case files • Constitution, Supreme Court & official exam data

Reservation is one of the most emotionally charged questions in the Republic. For some it is the unfinished promise of social justice; for others it looks like a system where birth still decides opportunity. Both views hold part of the truth — and the honest question is harder than "for or against."

"If reservation was designed to correct historical exclusion, how should the State decide when, where and for whom that corrective mechanism remains necessary in a rapidly changing India?"

File 01 — Why Reservation Was Necessary

Equal treatment doesn't fix an unequal starting line

Indian society was never organised around equal access to school, land, temples or public life. Untouchability and caste exclusion produced disadvantages a Constitution couldn't erase just by declaring citizens equal.

So the framers built in two ideas at once. Formal equality says everyone gets the same rule. Substantive equality says that sometimes unequal treatment is needed to correct deeply unequal starting conditions. Article 15(4) permits special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes and for SCs/STs; Article 15(6), added later, did the same for Economically Weaker Sections. The philosophy was never "one community is better" — it was "where historical exclusion produced unequal access, the State may intervene to create real equality of opportunity."

In simple words: substantive equality

If two people start from very different positions, giving them exactly the same treatment doesn't automatically give them an equal chance. Sometimes you have to treat people differently to make the outcome fair.

File 02 — Ambedkar & the "10-Year" Myth

What was actually meant to be temporary — and what wasn't

A common claim is that Ambedkar wanted reservation to last only ten years. That needs a correction: only political reservation of SC/ST seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies, under Article 334, had a time limit — and it has since been extended by constitutional amendment to eighty years from 1950. It was never true that all forms of reservation carried a ten-year sunset clause.

But a real question sits underneath the myth: should a corrective policy be periodically re-evaluated as society changes? That's a fair question regardless of the historical inaccuracy above it.

File 03 — Has India Changed Enough?

Enough to demand a rethink, not enough to declare victory

India of 2026 is not India of 1950 — education has expanded, cities have mixed communities that never used to mix, and untouchability is both illegal and increasingly socially unacceptable. Millions from historically excluded communities are now in universities, the bureaucracy and business.

But the paradox is real: reliable nationwide OBC data is still missing, because India hasn't run a full caste census since 1931 — so "how backward is backward now" is answered more by assumption than evidence in large parts of the system. That's precisely why the debate shouldn't be "reservation — yes or no," but "what form of affirmative action is justified by the inequalities that actually exist today."

India, 1950

  • Education access: Very limited
  • Untouchability: Legal exclusion still common
  • Urban-rural mixing: Minimal
  • Data on backwardness: 1931 Census (last full caste count)

India, 2026

  • Education access: Expanded, still unequal
  • Untouchability: Illegal, socially declining but not gone
  • Urban-rural mixing: Substantially increased
  • Data on backwardness: Still no full OBC caste census
File 04 — Two Students, Two Disadvantages

Both can be genuinely disadvantaged — for different reasons

Scarcity is where this debate gets personal. Government jobs and elite seats are limited, so any change in allocation creates real winners and real losers.

Student A

  • First-generation learner from a historically excluded community
  • Parents had little to no access to education
  • Reservation opens a door no one in the family has walked through before
  • Disadvantage is largely historical & social

Student B

  • First-generation learner from a poor family outside SC/ST/OBC
  • No family member has ever held a government job
  • Competes almost entirely through the open category, with little coaching access
  • Disadvantage is largely economic & educational

Neither student is a villain. Student B's frustration at losing a seat isn't automatically caste hatred — and it doesn't erase Student A's historical disadvantage either. Both realities can be true at once, which is exactly what political slogans tend to erase.

File 05 — What Do We Mean By Merit?

Qualifying isn't the same as competence — and the gap deserves scrutiny anyway

"Candidate B scored lower yet got the seat" is a real feature of category-specific cutoffs — but it doesn't establish incompetence. Entrance exams measure performance under one exam system, not ethics, creativity, perseverance or long-run professional skill. Still, if the gap becomes too large, it's fair to ask whether the pipeline before admission is doing its job.

What it does not mean: qualifying isn't the same as securing a seat, and a lower threshold doesn't measure a future doctor's or engineer's competence — both still complete the same curriculum, clinical training and professional exams after admission.

The sustainable fix isn't lowering standards indefinitely — it's strengthening the pipeline that comes before the exam: better schools → nutrition → teachers → coaching access → scholarships → fair admission → rigorous training.

File 06 — Can Equality Produce Inequality?

A policy built to fix inequality can still look unequal in practice

Reservation rests on substantive equality — but that idea has a danger of its own. If a category becomes a permanent political identity rather than a target on measurable disadvantage, a policy meant to correct inequality can start generating a new perception of it.

1

Generation 1 — first member of the family gets an opportunity: IAS, doctor, engineer, professor.

↓
2

Generation 2 — grows up with better schools, coaching, networks and financial security.

↓
3

Generation 3 — begins life in an already-established professional family.

Should policy treat all three generations identically? This is exactly why the Supreme Court has repeatedly pushed on the creamy-layer principle for OBCs — inclusion in backward-class status should rest on data, and failing to exclude the creamy layer undermines reservation's own logic. The legal position isn't identical everywhere, though: the Court's 2024 SC/ST sub-classification judgment noted that creamy-layer exclusion has historically applied to OBCs, not to SCs/STs in the same way.

In simple words: creamy layer

Once a family has already become financially and socially well-off, should its children still get the same reservation benefit meant for those who are still struggling? The creamy-layer rule tries to draw that line — for OBCs, by law.

File 07 — When Justice Becomes Political Capital

Genuine grievances, amplified by electoral math

India is an electoral democracy where caste identities still carry social and electoral weight. A party doesn't just compete for votes — it competes for social coalitions, and reservation can become politically valuable to promise or to oppose.

1

Social grievance — a community feels economically or educationally left behind.

↓
2

Community mobilisation turns the grievance into a public demand.

↓
3

Political parties negotiate, position and sometimes compete over the demand.

↓
4

The demand converts into a policy promise — and a consolidated vote bank.

The Maratha reservation movement in Maharashtra is a live example: the demand became a major electoral issue, with parties positioning around it while existing OBC leaders opposed Marathas entering the OBC quota — a dispute that is as much about the political economy of scarce seats as it is about backward-class criteria. None of this proves every reservation demand is politically manufactured. It shows that political actors can amplify and negotiate genuine social grievances — which is a very different claim.

File 08 — Abolish, Preserve or Redesign?

The honest options, laid out without a thumb on the scale

Three positions dominate the debate. Each has a real argument behind it — none of them is a strawman.

Abolish

  • Restores identical formal competition for all
  • Reduces long-term dependence on caste categories
  • Doesn't address individual economic disadvantage outside reserved categories

Preserve

  • Structural inequality and under-representation haven't disappeared
  • Untouchability's legacy is still measurable in access and outcomes
  • Removing it risks reversing genuine gains for first-generation families

Redesign

  • Periodic review using real social and educational data
  • Stronger, evidence-based creamy-layer targeting
  • Treats EWS and open-category fairness as part of the same problem

This report's analytical direction leans toward redesign — not as unquestionable truth, but as the position that takes both the historical case for reservation and its present-day limitations seriously at the same time.

01

Periodic Review

Re-assess categories against measurable social and educational data, not assumption.

02

Better Targeting

Route benefits to those still genuinely disadvantaged, not the already upwardly mobile.

03

Stronger Creamy Layer

Apply economic-and-social advancement tests wherever constitutionally appropriate.

04

Take EWS Seriously

Keep evaluating its eligibility criteria and real-world effectiveness.

05

Fix the Pipeline

Schools, nutrition, scholarships and coaching access — attack the cause, not just the symptom.

06

Expand the Pie

Create more good seats and jobs instead of only redividing the same 100.

Verdict: Justice Must Evolve With Society

Not "abolish" and not "reservation forever" — evidence-based, reviewed, targeted

Caste exclusion, untouchability and denial of educational opportunity were real, and the Constitution was right to confront them. Acknowledging that history doesn't require believing every part of the present system is beyond question. India has changed enormously — but economic deprivation still cuts across caste, and political parties have learned that caste and reservation are powerful tools of electoral mobilisation.

The danger was never reservation itself. It's when a mechanism built for social justice becomes dependent on political incentives for its own continuation and expansion. A mature democracy doesn't force citizens to choose between historical justice and contemporary fairness — it pursues periodic, evidence-based review of who still needs the corrective, alongside universal investment in education, health and opportunity for everyone.

Perhaps the most useful question for the next generation isn't "who deserves reservation" — it's "why does a modern India still need to depend so heavily on reservation to distribute opportunity in the first place?"
